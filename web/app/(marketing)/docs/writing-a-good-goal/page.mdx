# Writing a good goal

The goal is the entire product   not a figure of speech.

The same calling engine, given a one-line task, produces a confusing robocall people
hang up on. Given a well-written goal, it produces something closer to a competent
junior colleague. Nothing else you configure moves the outcome as much.

## A thin goal fails before it dials

The engine checks whether a task has enough substance to hold a conversation. This
one is rejected outright:

```text
Call and ask about their trip.
```

It comes back as `call_not_ready`. CallFlow enforces a 40-character minimum in the
campaign editor, so you find out while writing rather than mid-run.

The minimum is a floor, not a target. A goal that just clears it will dial   and
still go badly, because the agent has to improvise everything you didn't say.

## The four things a goal must state

A goal that works answers all four of these. In our experience the second and
fourth are the ones people leave out.

1. **Who is calling and why.** The agent needs to identify itself, name the
   organisation, and say what the call is about in one sentence.
2. **What to ask, in order.** Number them. An unordered list of topics produces a
   call that wanders and a result with holes in it.
3. **What to do when they say yes.** Confirm what? Offer what? Record what?
4. **What to do when they say no**   and separately, what to do when they say *not
   now*. These are different outcomes and conflating them is the most common
   mistake.

Everything beyond those four is refinement. Everything short of them is a gamble.

## Say what the agent must refuse to do

An agent that hasn't been told what's out of bounds will helpfully answer a question
it should have deferred   quoting a price, confirming a date, commenting on a medical
result   because being helpful is what it's for.

Write the boundary explicitly:

```text
Do not quote prices, offer a discount, or commit to any date. If they ask what it
costs, say pricing depends on volume, that it is published on the website, and that
a specialist will confirm the detail.
```

A deferred answer costs you a follow-up. A wrong answer on a recorded call costs
you more than that.

## Give the escalation instruction in the goal itself

The triage rules run over the result regardless, so frustration and opt-outs are
caught whether or not you mention them. But saying it in the goal changes the
agent's behaviour *during* the call rather than only classifying it afterwards:

```text
If at any point they sound frustrated, ask to speak to a person, or ask you to
stop, stop immediately, apologise once, and hand off.
```

Without that line the agent will often try one more question first. With it, it
stops.

## Ask whether now is a good time

One sentence, near the start, and it changes the shape of your results.

Without it, a contact caught at a bad moment produces a short, negative call that
looks like disinterest. With it, the same contact says "not now", the call is queued
for a polite retry, and you keep the lead. **A bad time is not a bad mood**   and
the difference only shows up in your data if the agent asked.

## Use variables for anything that differs per contact

`{name}` and any `{context[key]}` you supply are substituted before the call. Put the
specifics in context rather than writing one campaign per situation:

```text
You are calling {name} about the {context[role]} role at {context[company]}.
Confirm they can work from {context[location]}, or would need remote.
```

A missing context key renders as an empty string rather than failing the run, which
is forgiving at call time and easy to miss while authoring   so check the live
preview in the campaign editor with a real contact selected before you save.

## What a finished goal looks like

Long enough to be specific, ordered, with the refusals and the escalation stated. If
yours is three lines, it isn't finished   the starter templates run to twenty or
more, and that's why they work. Every solution page on this site shows its goal
template in full.

## Test it before you trust it

A dry run renders the goal with your actual contact substituted in and shows the
exact words they'd hear   at no cost and with nothing dialled.

Read that output aloud once. Goals that look fine on the page and sound wrong in the
mouth are common, and thirty seconds of reading catches almost all of them.
